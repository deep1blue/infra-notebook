SFT: Teaching the Model Its Manners

Pretraining builds a brilliant mind with no manners. Supervised fine-tuning is the short, careful schooling that turns a document-completer into an assistant — and the whole trick fits inside one masking decision.

SFT and RL Lesson

Builds on: pretraining — next-token prediction on raw internet text. One-line recap: a base model knows what text usually comes next. It has knowledge, but no manners and no notion of being helpful.

Watch what that means in practice. Hand a pure base model the prompt "write a haiku about GPUs" and it may emit three more prompts, or a forum argument about whether haikus can mention tensor cores — because on the internet, all of those are plausible continuations. The model is a brilliant improv partner with no script. Supervised fine-tuning hands it the script.

The deep intuition: shape, not facts

SFT teaches the shape of the job, not new facts. The landmark evidence is LIMA (2023) — researchers aligned a 65-billion-parameter model with just 1,000 carefully curated examples. A thousand. The knowledge was already in the weights from pretraining; SFT mostly elicits the behavior of being an assistant. When someone tells you fine-tuning "teaches the model new things," this is the counterexample to keep in your pocket.

The mechanism: four parts, one that interviewers probe

  1. The data: (instruction, response) pairs, often multi-turn conversations, where quality dominates quantity.
  2. The chat template: roles get wrapped in special tokens, e.g. <|user|>Explain GPUs<|end|><|assistant|>…. The model only ever trains on this exact format, so inference prompts must match it token for token — template mismatch is a real, silent performance killer.
  3. Loss masking: cross-entropy is computed only on the response tokens. Every prompt token gets label −100, the "ignore me" label. Why? Because training the model to predict the user's tokens would teach it to roleplay the user. You want P(response | instruction), never P(instruction).
  4. The regime: one to three epochs at a small learning rate, roughly 1–2e−5 — you are nudging settled weights, not rebuilding them — and often through LoRA or QLoRA when a full finetune costs too much.

The math that matters

The math is almost disappointingly simple, which is the point. It is the same autoregressive loss as pretraining, restricted to the response:

L = −Σt ∈ response log p(xt | x<t)

Nothing exotic. The masking is the whole trick — one decision about which tokens count is what separates "assistant" from "autocomplete."

Connects to: the next two nodes on the tree. Next is SFT data curation — why a thousand great examples beat a million mediocre ones, and how you keep test questions from leaking into training. After that comes the RLHF pipeline, which takes this SFT model as its starting policy and teaches it preferences. Every Llama chat model you have ever used walked exactly this path.

Your thirty-second interview answer "SFT converts a next-token predictor into an instruction follower by fine-tuning on instruction–response pairs with loss computed only on the response tokens. The LIMA result — a thousand curated examples aligning a 65B model — shows SFT teaches format and behavior, not new knowledge."

SFT Data: Why the Dataset Is the Product

Last night you drilled the SFT machine. This morning we look at what you feed it: a thousand perfect examples beat fifty thousand sloppy ones, and one contaminated eval can lie to your whole team for months.

SFT and RL Lesson

Builds on: B1 — SFT mechanics (instruction tuning, chat templates, loss masking). One-line recap: SFT is behavior cloning on assistant turns — cross-entropy computed only on response tokens — so it teaches the shape of assistance, not new knowledge. That recap is now the whole lesson: because SFT clones behavior, the dataset you clone from is the product.

Intuition: ten perfect laps

SFT is hiring a driving instructor for a student who already knows how cars work. Pretraining read every driving manual ever written; it just needs ten perfect demo laps — perfect, not a thousand sloppy ones, because a student copies whatever you demonstrate, flaws included.

The receipts: LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) aligned a 65B-parameter LLaMA with exactly 1,000 hand-curated examples — no RLHF, no preference modeling — and in a controlled human study its answers were equivalent to or preferred over GPT-4 in 43% of 300 test prompts, beating models trained on 52× more data. AlpaGasus (Chen et al., 2023) did the mirror experiment: it filtered Alpaca's 52k examples down to 9k with a strong LLM judge, and the 9k model beat the full 52k on every test set while training 5.7× faster (14 minutes instead of 80 on a 7B). The hypothesis that explains both: the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis — pretraining wrote the library; SFT only builds the card catalog pointing at the right subdistribution of formats.

Mechanism: the four-stage curation pipeline

  1. Sourcing — where examples come from. Human-authored (LIMA's 250 researcher-written examples: the gold standard, and the most expensive), distilled from stronger models (Alpaca's 52k from text-davinci-003), or synthetic (Self-Instruct, 2022: prompt a model with 175 seed tasks, generate instructions, filter, repeat — 52k instructions from almost nothing). Signature failures: humans don't scale, distillation inherits the teacher's flaws, synthetic data compounds errors if you loop it.
  2. Filtering — score and cut. The AlpaGasus move: ask a strong model to rate each example 1–5 and keep the top tier. The trap: the filter's biases become your model's biases — filter with a verbose judge and you get a verbose model (gotcha #8 from the SFT lesson, wearing a new costume). Filter for duplicates too: exact and near-dup removal, because duplicates overweight themselves in the gradient.
  3. Decontamination — the eval-integrity step. If your eval questions appear in training, your scores are fiction. The recipes: n-gram overlap removal (drop any training doc sharing a 13-gram with an eval item), canary strings (grep your outputs for BIG-bench's canary GUID), temporal cutoffs (train only on data older than the eval), embedding near-dup against eval sets.
  4. Mix design and formatting — apply the chat template in the pipeline itself (B1's silent killer returns: same template at train and serve, enforced where the data is built), and balance the mixture: task diversity plus the refusal boundary. Too many refusal examples → the over-refusal failure from last night's mock; too few → unsafe.

The math that matters

1. The normalization subtlety returns. Masked CE averages over response tokens only, so long responses dominate the gradient mix. Worked example: a batch of 10 examples, nine with 40-token responses, one with a 600-token response. Token total: 360 + 600 = 960. That single long example owns 62.5% of the gradient. If it's low-quality — and AlpaGasus found Alpaca's 52k was full of exactly these — one bad example steers the batch.

L = −(1/Nresp) · Σt ∈ response log p(xt | x<t)
same loss as B1 — the data decides what Nresp means

The interview-deployable rule: budget datasets in response tokens, not example counts, and monitor the token-length distribution, not the dataset size.

2. Why the 13-gram decontamination threshold works. With a ~50k vocabulary, a random 13-gram collision has probability on the order of 50k−13 — effectively zero. So any match is real contamination, not noise: the threshold has essentially no false positives by construction. That's why the industry converged on it.

3. The weakest-link law of coverage. Your miss rate is set by your least-covered capability. Ten thousand more coding examples don't fix zero medical-safety examples. Diversity isn't a vibe — it's the max over capabilities of the uncovered fraction, and LIMA's authors picked their 1,000 for prompt diversity first.

Connects to: the RLHF pipeline next — preference data needs the same curation discipline, and this SFT model becomes π_ref, so every bad example you let through is inherited by the KL anchor. Then reward modeling: Bradley–Terry on dirty preference pairs is the same disease one stage later. And decontamination gets its own node later: eval harnesses, where eval integrity is the whole subject.

🎯 Your 30-second interview answer

"SFT data is the product. LIMA aligned a 65B model with a thousand curated examples because SFT teaches the shape of assistance, not new knowledge — so you curate for diversity, response quality, and honesty: filter hard, dedup, decontaminate against your evals with n-gram overlap, and budget by response tokens rather than example counts, because masked loss lets long responses dominate the gradient mix."

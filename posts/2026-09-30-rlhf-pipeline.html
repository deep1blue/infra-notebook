RLHF: Teaching the Model What Humans Want

Yesterday you saw SFT's imitation ceiling. Today the ceiling becomes the floor: humans are slow writers but fast judges — so rank pairs of outputs, compress the rankings into a reward function, and optimize the policy against it at GPU speed.

SFT and RL Lesson

Builds on: B1 — SFT mechanics. Recap: SFT is behavior cloning with masked cross-entropy on assistant turns — it teaches what good looks like, but it cannot teach which of two good answers a human prefers. That gap is the entire reason RLHF exists. B2 — SFT data: demonstrations cost human-hours to write (LIMA's 1,000 took weeks of researcher time), while ranking two model outputs takes a labeler seconds. That cost asymmetry is the economic engine of today's lesson. Spaced retrieval, as promised: B1's SFT checkpoint gets a second job today as π_ref, the reference policy the RL objective is anchored to.

Intuition: the imitation ceiling, then the floor

SFT maximizes the likelihood of demonstrated tokens. Show it two different good answers to the same prompt and it can only average into them — there is no channel for "humans prefer A over B." SFT teaches format, not preference: the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis, one stage later.

But humans are noisy writers and reliable comparators. Asking a labeler to write the perfect answer costs minutes; asking them to rank four to nine candidate answers costs seconds and produces more signal per dollar. The InstructGPT receipts: ~13k hand-written demonstrations for the SFT stage, ~33k pairwise comparisons for the reward model. Preference data dwarfed demonstration data — because ranking scales and writing doesn't.

The central trick: a reward model is a preference compressor. Train it once on tens of thousands of rankings, then it scores millions of rollouts for free, overnight, at GPU speed. RLHF = compress human judgment into rθ, then optimize π against rθ. Sample prompt, generate, score, update, repeat — a million tiny judgments a human never has to make.

Mechanism: the three stages

  1. SFT. Masked cross-entropy on demonstrations gives π_SFT. This checkpoint now has two jobs: it initializes the policy πφ, and it becomes π_ref, the reference model the RL objective anchors to. Keep that anchor in mind — it is the most important model in the room.
  2. The reward model. Collect preference triples (x, yw, yl): a prompt plus a winning and a losing response. Assume Bradley–Terry: P(yw ≻ yl | x) = σ(rθ(x,yw) − rθ(x,yl)) — the probability the human prefers the winner is the sigmoid of the score margin. Train by maximizing that log-probability over the dataset: LBT = −E[log σ(Δr)]. Architecture: the SFT backbone with a scalar head — take the final token's hidden state, one linear layer, one number. The warning that matters: only margins mean anything. Rewards are defined up to an additive constant, so raw scores are not comparable across prompts — an interview trap worth remembering.
  3. PPO. The loop: sample a prompt x → roll out a response y ~ πφ (at sampling temperature, because diversity is the training signal) → score it with rθ → estimate advantages with the learned value head → update the policy with the clipped surrogate LCLIP = E[min(ρÂ, clip(ρ, 1−ε, 1+ε)Â)], where ρ = πφ/πold keeps steps small and safe. And the full objective carries the anchor: J(φ) = E[rθ(x,y)] − β·KL(πφ(·|x) ‖ π_ref(·|x)). InstructGPT ran this over ~31k prompts with β = 0.02.

The KL term: a trust region, not a regularizer

Drop the KL penalty and the policy optimizes the proxy as hard as it can. Proxies have blind spots, and a strong optimizer finds every one of them: responses get verbose, hedged, sycophantic, confidently wrong — reward hacking, Goodhart's law wearing a lab coat. The KL penalty is not a regularizer; it is a trust region on the reward model's domain of competence. The SFT checkpoint is the territory where the reward model's judgments were earned; β sets how far the policy may wander from home. Without rent, the policy moves into the wilderness where the judge is blind and scores are hallucinations. When an interviewer asks "why the KL penalty," that sentence is your answer.

The math that matters

1. The Bradley–Terry loss — cross-entropy over the margin's sigmoid:

LBT = −E[log σ(rθ(x,yw) − rθ(x,yl))]

2. The RL objective with the anchor — KL estimated per-token from the rollouts themselves:

J(φ) = E[rθ] − β·KL(πφ ‖ πref)
KL = Ey~πφ[log πφ(y|x) − log πref(y|x)]

3. The InstructGPT receipts — 13k demonstrations → SFT; 33k comparisons → reward model; 31k prompts → PPO; β = 0.02. The ratio to remember is 13k : 33k: preference data outscored demonstration data more than two-to-one, because ranking is the scalable human input.

Failure modes preview — tonight's drill territory

Verbosity bias (longer answers score higher for no reason), distribution shift (the policy explores where the reward model never trained), and labeler disagreement (whose preferences are these, exactly?). And remember last night's reward-shaping field guide — length penalties, KL terms, verifiable rewards: that guide is the catalog of countermeasures for exactly this fight, and B4 gives shaping its own section.

🎯 Your 30-second interview answer

"Walk me through the RLHF pipeline." → "Three stages. First, supervised fine-tune to get a competent assistant — that checkpoint becomes both the starting policy and the KL anchor. Second, have humans rank pairs of model outputs — ranking is far cheaper than writing demonstrations — and train a reward model on those rankings with the Bradley–Terry loss so it predicts human preference. Third, run PPO: sample prompts, generate rollouts, score them with the reward model, and update the policy to maximize reward minus a KL penalty against the SFT model. The KL term is the load-bearing part — without it the policy games the reward model, which is reward hacking. With it, you get a model optimized for what humans prefer, not just what it was shown."
